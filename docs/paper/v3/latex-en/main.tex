\begin{abstract}
Container terminals aim at improving the efficiency of yard and gate
operations by shaping where and when truck jobs are served. Given that
external truck jobs are assigned to a yard block and an appointment time
before the truck arrives, the ability to revise those assignments as
congestion develops is a critical component for terminal operations.
In this study, a reinforcement learning architecture that jointly
revisits the yard block and the arrival time of an already announced
truck job is proposed. A significant challenge is to cope with the
indirect effects of a reallocation: moving one job changes the waiting
of the following trucks, the crane work sequence, and the vessel supply
flow, so the immediate travel time alone does not reveal whether the
action was worthwhile. We employ a counterfactual, simulation-based cost
signal to tackle this challenge. Our novel idea is to separate the
policy that requests a reallocation from the policy that accepts it on
behalf of the destination block or time slot, and to let a central
procedure commit only the candidates on which both agree under resource
constraints. Each policy is trained on the cost difference between
discrete-event simulations that differ in a single action from the same
state, whereas inference at operating time uses the trained networks
alone. The efficacy of the proposed method is verified by numerical
experiments based on a continuous synthetic environment whose terminal
size, time-of-day truck arrivals, multiple yard cranes, and vessel call
volumes follow ranges reported in the literature. The results indicate
that the decision path operates stably throughout continuous operation,
that the cost reduction is concentrated in arrival-time adjustment under
congested conditions, and that the trained model attains lower cost than
rule-based policies matched to the same action range.

\keywords{Smart port \and Container terminal \and Truck arrival
management \and Yard block reallocation \and Counterfactual learning
\and Reinforcement learning}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Operational efficiency at a smart port depends not only on equipment
automation but also on the ability to reallocate resources from
information observed in real time, and smart-port research connects
equipment and vehicle information to operating
scenarios~\cite{ref1}. At a container terminal the
working block and appointment time of an external truck are fixed before
arrival, yet during operation demand concentrates on particular slots or
blocks and crane queues change with it, so keeping the initial plan
makes it difficult to reflect newly observed congestion. This paper
revisits both \emph{where} a job is placed and \emph{when} the truck is
asked to arrive.

Truck appointment research uses time windows to spread arrival peaks and
lets a terminal and carriers negotiate arrival times against their own
costs~\cite{ref4,ref6}, while yard operations research addresses
placement, work sequencing, and crane interference~\cite{ref7,ref8,ref9}.
The two streams have improved temporal and spatial efficiency
separately; this paper places both axes inside one real-time decision.
Such a reallocation does not change the cost of a single job alone: when
a job moves, the waiting of following trucks, the crane work sequence,
and vessel supply flow change as well, so a cost signal including the
induced congestion is required, and difference rewards and multi-agent
counterfactual baselines provide one by comparing an action with an
alternative~\cite{ref10,ref11}. Reallocation is also difficult to commit
on the proposing side's benefit alone, since whether the destination can
absorb the job must be judged separately; we therefore separate proposal
and acceptance and leave consent and feasibility to a central
procedure.

We ask whether spatio-temporal reallocation can be composed from
independent proposal and acceptance policies, whether counterfactual
learning can be separated from operational inference, whether the
architecture reduces operating cost, and whether the roles of learning
and of the action range can be told apart in the observed change. The
contributions are an \emph{independent policy structure}, a
\emph{spatio-temporal action range}, \emph{counterfactual cost
learning}, a \emph{separation of learning and operation}, and a
\emph{decomposable evaluation} that reports execution, action range, and
learning effect separately.

\section{Related Work}

Smart-port research treats automation, intelligence, and environmental
design as distinct components of port efficiency~\cite{ref1}. Time-window
appointments spread concentrated truck arrivals~\cite{ref4}, and
negotiation studies show that carriers and a terminal can adjust arrival
times while exchanging limited information~\cite{ref6}, and arrivals are
not uniform over the day~\cite{ref13}. On the spatial side, yard crane research addresses
work sequencing, interference, and load balancing~\cite{ref7,ref8};
simulation compares how block length and crane deployment affect long-run
performance~\cite{ref9}; and the ranking of dispatching rules can reverse
with terminal conditions~\cite{ref16}. Reinforcement learning in ports
has reached real-time crane dispatching~\cite{ref19}; this paper
addresses the layer above it.

Difference rewards isolate the effect of one action from a joint cost by
comparing it against a baseline in which other agents keep their
actions~\cite{ref10}, and counterfactual multi-agent policy gradients
construct such a baseline from a centralised critic~\cite{ref11}. We
apply this to discrete-event simulation and use the monetary cost
difference between two runs differing in a single action as the
regression target of each independent policy. A related use of machine
learning for resource-level control under environmental uncertainty is
found in building demand response~\cite{ref20}; that study likewise
combines measured environmental records with synthetic parameters for
the variation across individual units and states the synthetic component
declaratively, an approach we follow in Sect.~3.1.
For policy comparison we use paired runs sharing common random
numbers~\cite{ref22}.

What distinguishes this work is the combination of an action range
covering both arrival-time adjustment and block reallocation,
independent proposal and acceptance policies, commitment based on mutual
consent, and the separation of counterfactual learning from operational
inference within one architecture.
