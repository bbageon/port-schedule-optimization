\begin{abstract}
Container terminals aim at improving the efficiency of yard and gate
operations by shaping where and when truck jobs are served. Given that
external truck jobs are assigned to a yard block and an appointment time
before the truck arrives, the ability to revise those assignments as
congestion develops is a critical component for terminal operations.
In this study, a reinforcement learning architecture that jointly
revisits the yard block and the arrival time of an already announced
truck job is proposed. A significant challenge is to cope with the
indirect effects of a reallocation: moving one job changes the waiting
of the following trucks, the crane work sequence, and the vessel supply
flow, so the immediate travel time alone does not reveal whether the
action was worthwhile. We employ a counterfactual, simulation-based cost
signal to tackle this challenge. Our novel idea is to separate the
policy that requests a reallocation from the policy that accepts it on
behalf of the destination block or time slot, and to let a central
procedure commit only the candidates on which both agree under resource
constraints. Each policy is trained on the cost difference between
discrete-event simulations that differ in a single action from the same
state, whereas inference at operating time uses the trained networks
alone, so that the expensive branch simulation never enters the
operational path. The efficacy of the proposed method is verified by
numerical experiments based on a continuous synthetic environment whose
terminal size, time-of-day truck arrivals, multiple yard cranes, and
vessel call volumes follow ranges reported in the literature. Under a
random seed not used for training, the proposed policy is compared
against no reallocation, rule-based policies, and the model before
training. The results indicate that the decision path operates stably
throughout continuous operation, that the direction of cost reduction is
concentrated in arrival-time adjustment under congested conditions, and
that the trained model attains lower cost than rule-based policies
matched to the same action range.

\keywords{Smart port \and Container terminal \and Truck arrival

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Background}
Operational efficiency at a smart port depends not only on equipment
automation but also on the ability to reallocate resources from
information observed in real time. Studies of port efficiency describe
automation, intelligence, and environmental design as separate channels
through which performance is affected, and smart-port programmes at
Busan and Singapore likewise aim to connect equipment and vehicle
information to operating scenarios~\cite{ref1,ref2,ref3}.

At a container terminal the working block and appointment time of an
external truck are normally fixed before arrival. During operation,
however, demand concentrates on particular time slots or blocks, and
crane queues and vessel-linked work change with it. Keeping the initial
plan unchanged makes it difficult to reflect newly observed congestion
in the assignment. This paper revisits, at that point, both
\emph{where} a job is placed and \emph{when} the truck is asked to
arrive.

\subsection{Problem Setting and Research Gap}
Truck appointment research uses time windows to spread arrival peaks and
has developed methods by which a terminal and carriers negotiate arrival
times against their own costs~\cite{ref4,ref5,ref6}. Yard operations
research addresses container placement, work sequencing, and
interference among multiple cranes in order to reduce waiting and
rehandling~\cite{ref7,ref8,ref9}. The two streams have improved
temporal and spatial efficiency separately. This paper places both axes
inside one real-time decision for jobs that have already been announced.

Spatio-temporal reallocation does not change the cost of a single job
alone. When a job moves to another block or time, the waiting of
following trucks, the crane work sequence, and vessel supply flow change
as well. Evaluating an action by its immediate travel time is therefore
insufficient; a cost signal that includes the induced congestion is
required. Difference rewards and multi-agent counterfactual baselines
provide a way to isolate the contribution of one action by comparing it
with an alternative~\cite{ref10,ref11}.

Reallocation is also difficult to commit on the benefit of the proposing
side alone. Whether the destination block or target time slot can absorb
the job must be judged separately. Rather than a single central policy
that makes every decision, we separate proposal and acceptance into
independent policies and leave consent and resource feasibility to a
central procedure.

\subsection{Research Questions and Contributions}
Four research questions are addressed in this study.
\textbf{RQ1.}~Can spatio-temporal reallocation be composed from
independent proposal and acceptance policies?
\textbf{RQ2.}~Can counterfactual learning be separated from operational
inference?
\textbf{RQ3.}~Does the proposed architecture show potential for
operating-cost reduction?
\textbf{RQ4.}~Can the roles of learning and of the action range be
separated in the observed change?

The contributions of this work are fivefold. First, an \emph{independent
policy structure} separates proposal from acceptance and commits only
mutually agreed jobs. Second, a \emph{spatio-temporal action range}
treats block reallocation and arrival-time adjustment within one
decision procedure. Third, \emph{counterfactual cost learning} captures
indirect congestion effects from simulations differing in a single
action. Fourth, a \emph{separation of learning and operation} confines
the expensive branch simulation to training. Fifth, a
\emph{decomposable evaluation} reports the execution, the action range,
and the learning effect separately, so that the source of an observed
change can be identified.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Smart Ports and Truck Arrival-Time Adjustment}
Smart-port research treats automation, intelligence, and environmental
design as distinct components of port efficiency~\cite{ref1}. The Busan
digital twin and the Tuas Port programme illustrate how equipment,
vehicle, and space information is connected to operating scenarios and
to automated equipment control~\cite{ref2,ref3}. Time-window
appointments are a representative approach to spreading concentrated
truck arrivals~\cite{ref4}, and negotiation studies show that carriers
and a terminal can adjust arrival times while exchanging only limited
information~\cite{ref5,ref6}. A vehicle appointment pilot at Busan
likewise indicates that time adjustment can translate into on-site queue
management~\cite{ref12}. Truck arrivals are not uniform over the
day~\cite{ref13}, and the arrival pattern itself changes turn
time~\cite{ref14}.

\subsection{Yard Operations and Reinforcement Learning}
Yard crane research addresses work sequencing, interference among
cranes, and load balancing across blocks~\cite{ref7,ref8}. Dynamic
discrete-event simulation has been used to compare how block length and
crane deployment affect long-run terminal
performance~\cite{ref9,ref15}. Recent work shows that the performance
ranking of dispatching rules can reverse with terminal
conditions~\cite{ref16}, and configurations with multiple cranes per
block are studied as integrated scheduling
problems~\cite{ref17}. Because equipment throughput depends on move type
and interference, move units and truck units must be reported
separately~\cite{ref18}. Reinforcement learning in ports has been
extended to real-time crane dispatching~\cite{ref19}; this paper instead
addresses the layer above it, reallocating the block and time of truck
jobs.

\subsection{Counterfactual Cost and Evaluation Design}
Difference rewards isolate the effect of one action from a joint cost by
comparing the outcome against a baseline in which other agents keep
their actions~\cite{ref10}, and counterfactual multi-agent policy
gradients construct such a baseline from a centralised critic
\cite{ref11}. We apply this idea to discrete-event simulation and use the
monetary cost difference between two runs differing in a single action
as the regression target of each independent policy. A related use of
machine learning for resource-level control under environmental
uncertainty is found in building demand response, where policies for
individual spaces are learned across tasks that share
structure~\cite{ref20}; that study likewise combines measured
environmental records with synthetic parameters for the variation across
individual units, and states the synthetic component
declaratively---an approach we follow in Sect.~3.1. RUDDER, which
redistributes delayed rewards over a trajectory~\cite{ref21}, is left as
a direction for extending long-horizon attribution. For policy
comparison we use paired runs sharing common random numbers, a
long-standing technique for reducing the noise of policy
differences~\cite{ref22}. Evidence that vessel call size creates
operational peaks also motivates evaluating several demand
levels~\cite{ref23}.

What distinguishes this work is the combination of an action range
covering both arrival-time adjustment and block reallocation,
independent proposal and acceptance policies, commitment based on mutual
consent, and the separation of counterfactual learning from operational
inference within one architecture.
