\section{Numerical Experiments}\label{S:NumericalExper}

\subsection{Scope of the Results}\label{S:ScopeoftheResu}
The results below come from the middle 28 days of a 30-day continuous
diagnostic run generated with random seed 9{,}900{,}950, which does not
overlap with training. Weights were frozen and the exploration
probability set to zero, and all policies on a given day shared the same
truck, vessel, and service random streams. Each day stores the four cost
terms, the demand level, the transaction types, the unfinished jobs, and
the long waits; the source code, the per-policy raw results, and the
scripts that generate every figure are released with the paper, so that
the seeds, checkpoints, and commands can be replayed.

\subsection{Confirmation of Architectural Operation}\label{S:Confirmationof}
Six structural conditions were confirmed by different paths. Separate
models for proposal and acceptance, the absence of any commitment on
one-sided consent, the exclusion of realised future arrival and
completion times from policy inputs, and the absence of any state reset
at the day boundary follow from the implementation, the commitment
constraints, and automated tests. Two were confirmed in the runs
themselves: counterfactual simulation calls during frozen-policy
evaluation numbered
\textbf{zero}, and the identity check---a branch that changes no action
reproducing the action and cost of the main run---passed 4/4 in the
training run, where counterfactual labels exist.

\subsection{Demand Level and the Decomposition of Actions}
Note that 90.5\% of the total cost reduction occurred on the four days
with demand of 12{,}500 and 15{,}000 trucks: KRW 0.611\,bn on the former
(41.5\% of the total) and 0.722\,bn on the latter (49.0\%), against
0.047, 0.060, and 0.032\,bn on the 12, 8, and 4 days of 3{,}500,
5{,}000, and 7{,}500 trucks. This supports developing the architecture
towards a policy that \emph{intervenes selectively when congestion is
observed} rather than continuously. To assess the contribution of each
action, only one of the two was then allowed over the same 28 days
(Table~\ref{tab:actions}, Fig.~\ref{fig:actions}). Arrival-time adjustment reduced cost by KRW
1.320\,bn on the four congested days and accounted for 91.4\% of the
total reduction. The block-only condition reduced cost by KRW
0.124\,bn under smooth and normal demand but increased cost at the
highest demand, leaving a net increase of KRW 0.131\,bn. The policy can
therefore be optimised towards adjusting the relative use of spatial and
temporal actions with the demand level. Time-slot quotas were open in
this diagnostic run, so the arrival-time figures describe the
\emph{range} of what time shifting could provide.

\subsection{Comparison with Rule-Based Policies of the Same Action Range}\label{S:Comparisonwith}
Sect.~5.5 raises the question of what a rule-based policy achieves when
given the same action. Two such policies were added by transferring the
\emph{least-loaded} criterion from the spatial to the temporal axis. No
new parameter is introduced, only the axis on which the criterion
applies, and they share the congestion condition of the other rules.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Cost reduction rate over 28 days by action range, with paired
comparisons}
\label{tab:width}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llr@{}}
\toprule
Policy & Action range & Reduction \\
\midrule
Proposed policy & Block + arrival time & \textbf{+14.72\%} \\
Proposed policy (arrival time only) & Arrival time & +13.44\% \\
Untrained model & Block + arrival time & +7.97\% \\
\textbf{Rule: least-loaded time slot} & Arrival time & \textbf{+3.41\%} \\
\textbf{Rule: least-loaded block and slot} & Block + arrival time & \textbf{+1.51\%} \\
Rule: least slack & Block & +0.53\% \\
Rule: first-come-first-served & Block & +0.17\% \\
Proposed policy (block only) & Block & $-$1.31\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Three observations can be made. Arrival-time adjustment lowers cost
significantly even under a rule ($+3.41\%$, $p=0.0125$), so the action
itself carries value; both rules also beat no reallocation (21/28,
$p=0.0125$ and 23/28, $p=0.0009$). When the action range is matched the
proposed policy costs less than the rule (20/28, $p=0.0357$; 14.72\%
against 1.51\% in total). Yet on the arrival-time axis alone the
\emph{rule was lower on more days} (the proposed policy 8/28) while the
total was about four times lower for the proposed policy.

The demand-level decomposition explains the third observation. Under
smooth and normal demand the rule is marginally lower ($-0.013$ against
$0.006$\,bn at 3{,}500 trucks), whereas on the four congested days the
proposed policy is 3.8--5.6 times lower (0.747 against 0.133\,bn at
15{,}000). The learned selection was optimised \emph{not towards winning
frequently but towards reducing cost sharply on the expensive days}, and
both indicators must be reported for this to be visible. In the same
decomposition the rule using both actions falls to 0.038 and
0.009\,bn under congested demand, reproducing the property of
Sect.~5.5---that block reallocation raises cost under congestion---and
suggesting it belongs to the action rather than to a particular policy.

\subsection{Learning Effect and Dispatching Sensitivity}\label{S:LearningEffect}
From KRW 10.00\,bn without reallocation, the untrained model reached KRW
9.20\,bn and the trained model KRW 8.52\,bn, so the additional reduction
after training is KRW 0.67\,bn, about 45\% of the total. On a daily
basis the trained model was lower on 18 of 28 days ($p=0.1849$): both the
amount and the win count point towards lower cost, but statistical
certainty is limited in the present sample, and because the large
differences concentrate on a few congested days, repeated evaluation with
more such days would delineate the learning contribution more sharply.
Separately, the ranking of crane dispatching rules reversed with
demand---the shortest-processing-time rule in current use was 22--25\%
more expensive than others under smooth and normal demand but the
cheapest from 7{,}500 trucks upward, and lowest overall when weighted by
the designed demand mix---so the effect of the reallocation policy should
also be re-examined under other dispatching rules.
