\section{Numerical Experiments}

\subsection{Scope of the Results}
The results below come from the middle 28 days of a 30-day continuous
diagnostic run generated with random seed 9{,}900{,}950, which does not
overlap with training. Weights were frozen and the exploration
probability set to zero, and all policies on a given day shared the same
truck, vessel, and service random streams. The results indicate the
potential of the architecture as observed in a literature-calibrated
synthetic environment.

\subsection{Confirmation of Architectural Operation}
Structural operation was confirmed on six conditions, whose confirmation
paths differ. Separate models for proposal and acceptance, and the
absence of any commitment on one-sided consent, follow from the
implementation and from constraint~(7). That no realised future arrival
or completion time entered a policy input was confirmed by
implementation review with the information-grade marking of each policy,
and that inventory, queues, and unfinished jobs are not reset at the day
boundary is confirmed by automated tests. Two conditions were confirmed
in the runs themselves: the number of counterfactual simulation calls
during frozen-policy evaluation was \textbf{zero} in the evaluation run,
and the identity check---a branch that changes no action reproducing the
action and cost of the main run---passed 4/4 in the training run, where
counterfactual labels exist. The evaluation run records counterfactual
calls, policy exceptions, and failed commitments in its result file, so
run-dependent conditions are confirmed by the run itself.

\subsection{Cost Direction by Demand Level}
Note that 90.5\% of the total cost reduction occurred on the four days
with demand of 12{,}500 and 15{,}000 trucks: KRW 0.611\,bn on the former
(41.5\% of the total) and 0.722\,bn on the latter (49.0\%), against
0.047, 0.060, and 0.032\,bn on the 12, 8, and 4 days of 3{,}500,
5{,}000, and 7{,}500 trucks. The per-demand totals appear in the
first column of Table~\ref{tab:actions}.
This observation supports developing the architecture towards a policy
that \emph{intervenes selectively when congestion is observed} rather
than continuously.
Increasing the number of congested repetitions would also raise the
statistical certainty of the effect size.

\subsection{Decomposition into Block and Arrival-Time Actions}
To assess the contribution of each action, only one of the two actions
was allowed over the same 28 days (Table~\ref{tab:actions}).
Arrival-time adjustment reduced cost by KRW
1.320\,bn on the four congested days and accounted for 91.4\% of the
total reduction. The block-only condition
reduced cost by KRW 0.124\,bn under smooth and normal demand but
increased cost at the highest demand, leaving a net increase of KRW
0.131\,bn. This decomposition indicates that the policy can be optimised
towards adjusting the relative use of spatial and temporal actions with
the demand level.

Time-slot quotas were open in this diagnostic run. The arrival-time

provide, and a sensitivity analysis with finite quotas would refine the
scope of application.

\subsection{Comparison with Rule-Based Policies of the Same Action Range}
Sect.~5.5 showed that most of the reduction comes from arrival-time
adjustment, which raises the question of what a rule-based policy
achieves when it is given the same action. To answer it, two rule-based
policies were added by transferring the \emph{least-loaded} criterion
already used on the spatial axis to the temporal axis. No new parameter is introduced---only the axis on which
the criterion is applied changes---and the policies share the same
congestion condition as the other rule-based policies.

Three observations can be made. First, arrival-time adjustment lowers
cost significantly even under a rule ($+3.41\%$, $p=0.0125$), which
indicates that the action itself carries value. Second, when the action
range is matched, the proposed policy costs less than the rule-based
policy (20/28, $p=0.0357$; 14.72\% against 1.51\% in total). Third, on
the arrival-time axis alone the \emph{rule-based policy was lower on
more days} (the proposed policy 8/28), yet the total was about four
times lower for the proposed policy.

The reason for the third observation lies in the demand-level
decomposition. Under smooth and normal demand the rule is marginally
lower (at 3{,}500 trucks, $-0.013$\,bn against $0.006$\,bn), whereas on
the four congested days the proposed policy is 3.8--5.6 times lower (at
15{,}000 trucks, 0.747\,bn against 0.133\,bn). The learned selection was
optimised \emph{not towards winning frequently but towards reducing cost
sharply on the expensive days}. Both indicators must be reported for
this property to be visible.

In the same decomposition the rule using both actions falls to 0.038\,bn
and 0.009\,bn under congested demand. The property observed in
Sect.~5.5---that block reallocation raises cost under congestion---is
reproduced in the same direction under a rule-based policy. This
suggests it is a property of the action itself rather than of a
particular policy.

\subsection{Learning Effect and Dispatching Sensitivity}
From KRW 10.00\,bn without reallocation, the untrained model reached KRW
9.20\,bn and the trained model KRW 8.52\,bn. The additional reduction
after training is KRW 0.67\,bn, about 45\% of the total reduction. On a
daily basis the trained model was lower on 18 of 28 days with a
two-sided sign test of $p=0.1849$. Both the amount and the win count
point in the direction of lower cost, but statistical certainty is
limited in the present sample. Because the large differences concentrate
on a small number of congested days, repeated evaluation with more
congested days would delineate the range of the learning contribution
more sharply.

The relative ranking of crane dispatching rules reversed with demand. The shortest-processing-time rule in current use
showed 22--25\% higher cost than other rules under smooth and normal
demand but was the lowest from 7{,}500 trucks upward. Weighted by the
designed demand mix, the 30-day expected cost is lowest for the current
rule. This indicates that the effect of the reallocation policy should
also be re-examined under different dispatching rules.
