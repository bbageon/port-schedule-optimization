\section{Results}

\subsection{Scope of the Results}
The results below come from the middle 28 days of a 30-day continuous
diagnostic run generated with random seed 9{,}900{,}950, which does not
overlap with training. Weights were frozen and the exploration
probability set to zero, and all policies on a given day shared the same
truck, vessel, and service random streams. The results indicate the
potential of the architecture as observed in a literature-calibrated
synthetic environment.

\subsection{Confirmation of Architectural Operation}
Structural operation was confirmed on six conditions. Because the
confirmation path differs by condition, we state the source of each.
The performance figures in Sect.~5.3 onward all come from the evaluation
run, whereas some of the conditions below were confirmed by automated
tests or by the training run.

That the proposal and the acceptance are judged by different models
follows from the implementation structure, and that no reallocation was
committed on one-sided consent is guaranteed by the constraint in
(7). That no realised future arrival or completion time entered a policy
input was confirmed by implementation review together with the
information-grade marking of each policy. That the number of
counterfactual simulation calls during frozen-policy evaluation is
\textbf{zero} was confirmed in the \emph{evaluation run}. The identity
check---in which a branch that changes no action reproduces the action
and cost of the main run---passed 4/4 in the \emph{training run}; the
check does not apply at evaluation, where no counterfactual label is
produced. That inventory, queues, and unfinished jobs are not reset at
the day boundary is confirmed by automated tests.

These conditions indicate that the separation of proposal, acceptance,
commitment, and execution from counterfactual learning and operational
inference held along a single execution path. The evaluation run now
records the number of counterfactual calls, policy exceptions, and
failed commitments in its result file, so in subsequent runs the
run-dependent conditions are confirmed by the run itself.

\subsection{Cost Direction by Demand Level}
90.5\% of the total cost reduction occurred on the four days with demand
of 12{,}500 and 15{,}000 trucks (Table~\ref{tab:byload}). This supports
developing the architecture towards a policy that \emph{intervenes
selectively when congestion is observed} rather than continuously.
Increasing the number of congested repetitions would also raise the
statistical certainty of the effect size.

\subsection{Decomposition into Block and Arrival-Time Actions}
Over the same 28 days we allowed only one of the two actions
(Table~\ref{tab:actions}, Fig.~\ref{fig:actions}). Arrival-time
adjustment reduced cost by KRW 1.320\,bn on the four congested days and
accounted for 91.4\% of the total reduction. The block-only condition
reduced cost by KRW 0.124\,bn under smooth and normal demand but
increased cost at the highest demand, leaving a net increase of KRW
0.131\,bn. This decomposition indicates that the policy can be optimised
towards adjusting the relative use of spatial and temporal actions with
the demand level.

\subsection{Comparison with Rule-Based Policies of the Same Action Range}
Section~5.5 showed that most of the reduction comes from arrival-time
adjustment. We therefore ask what happens when a rule-based policy uses
the same action. We added two rule-based policies by transferring the
\emph{least-loaded} criterion already used on the spatial axis to the
temporal axis. No new parameter is introduced---only the axis on which
the criterion is applied changes---and the policies share the same
congestion condition as the other rule-based policies.

Three observations follow. First, arrival-time adjustment lowers cost
significantly even under a rule ($+3.41\%$, $p=0.0125$): the action
itself carries value. Second, when the action range is matched, the
proposed policy costs less than the rule-based policy (20/28,
$p=0.0357$; 14.72\% against 1.51\% in total). Third, on the arrival-time
axis alone the \emph{rule-based policy was lower on more days} (the
proposed policy 8/28), yet the total was about four times lower for the
proposed policy.

The reason for the third observation lies in the demand-level
decomposition. Under smooth and normal demand the rule is marginally
lower (at 3{,}500 trucks, $-0.013$\,bn against $0.006$\,bn), whereas on
the four congested days the proposed policy is 3.8--5.6 times lower (at
15{,}000 trucks, 0.747\,bn against 0.133\,bn). The learned selection was
optimised \emph{not towards winning frequently but towards reducing cost
sharply on the expensive days}. Both indicators must be reported for
this property to be visible.

In the same decomposition the rule using both actions falls to 0.038\,bn
and 0.009\,bn under congested demand. The property observed in
Sect.~5.5---that block reallocation raises cost under congestion---is
reproduced in the same direction under a rule-based policy. This
suggests it is a property of the action itself rather than of a
particular policy.

\subsection{Learning Effect and Dispatching Sensitivity}
From KRW 10.00\,bn without reallocation, the untrained model reached KRW
9.20\,bn and the trained model KRW 8.52\,bn. The additional reduction
after training is KRW 0.67\,bn, about 45\% of the total reduction. On a
daily basis the trained model was lower on 18 of 28 days with a
two-sided sign test of $p=0.1849$. Both the amount and the win count
point in the direction of lower cost, but statistical certainty is
limited in the present sample. Because the large differences concentrate
on a small number of congested days, repeated evaluation with more
congested days would delineate the range of the learning contribution
more sharply.

The relative ranking of crane dispatching rules reversed with demand
(Fig.~\ref{fig:crane}). The shortest-processing-time rule in current use
showed 22--25\% higher cost than other rules under smooth and normal
demand but was the lowest from 7{,}500 trucks upward. Weighted by the
designed demand mix, the 30-day expected cost is lowest for the current
rule. This indicates that the effect of the reallocation policy should
also be re-examined under different dispatching rules.

\subsection{Observations by Research Question}
For RQ1, reallocation that passed mutual consent and capacity checking
was executed in a continuous environment. For RQ2, decisions were made
from two cost models with no counterfactual call during evaluation. For
RQ3, a direction of lower cost than no reallocation was observed on most
days. For RQ4, the cost change concentrated on arrival-time adjustment
and on congested days; lower cost than a rule-based policy matched on
action range was observed (20/28, $p=0.0357$), while the comparison with
the untrained model pointed in the same direction with limited certainty
in the present sample.
