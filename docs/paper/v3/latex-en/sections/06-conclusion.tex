\section{Reproduction}

Reproduction separates the random seeds of training and evaluation and
lets all policies on a given evaluation day share the truck demand,
vessel schedule, and service random streams. Policy weights and the
comparison conditions are frozen before evaluation. For each day we
store not only the total cost but also the cost terms, the demand level,
the transaction types, the unfinished jobs, and the long waits.

The procedure is as follows. We first check the terminal, arrival,
vessel, and cost constants together with the policy input dimensions,
the counterfactual horizon, and the optimisation settings. The 30-day
demand plan is printed in advance to confirm that the first and last day
serve as connecting days. The policy is then trained in the diagnostic
seed band with per-day checkpoints and losses stored. Next, a 30-day run
per policy is executed with frozen weights under a seed that does not
overlap training, and the middle 28 days are paired by date to compute
total cost, median difference, and the sign test. Finally, the block and
time actions are restricted one at a time to decompose the source of the
cost change.

Seven checks are automated: no commitment lacking either a proposal or
an acceptance; no re-review of an already reviewed job; no realised
future arrival or completion time in a policy input; a branch that
changes no action reproducing the action and cost of the main run; the
four cost terms using the same day boundary; zero counterfactual calls
in frozen-policy evaluation; and no reset of inventory, queues, or
unfinished jobs at the day boundary.

A reproduction report should state the random seeds, checkpoints, code
commit, and run commands; the daily demand levels and per-policy total
costs; the four cost terms; the number of committed transactions and
their action types; the number of unfinished jobs and the distribution
of long waits; the daily win counts, median differences, and two-sided
sign-test $p$ values; and the comparison of the untrained model, the
trained model, and no reallocation under identical conditions.

\section{Conclusion}

\subsection{Summary}
This paper proposed a reinforcement learning architecture that jointly
revisits the yard block and arrival time of external truck jobs in
response to real-time congestion at a container terminal. The
reallocation proposal policy and the acceptance policy are modelled
independently, and a central commitment procedure applies the
assignments in one batch after checking mutual consent and resource
constraints. During training, counterfactual costs are produced by
discrete-event simulations that differ in a single action; at operating
time, candidates are evaluated by the trained networks alone. The
construction connects truck arrival-time negotiation~\cite{ref2,ref3},
yard crane operations~\cite{ref4,ref5}, and multi-agent counterfactual
learning~\cite{ref7,ref8} into one spatio-temporal assignment problem.

\subsection{Academic Implications}
First, spatial placement and temporal adjustment are compared within one
action range rather than treated as separate problems, so the policy can
consider both where congestion occurs and when it is expected. Second,
the roles of proposing and accepting are separated: instead of one
policy deciding on behalf of the whole terminal, the sending and
receiving sides each judge from limited observations while the central
procedure checks only consent and feasibility. This extends the
operating logic of distributed appointment negotiation to a real-time
reallocation policy~\cite{ref2,ref3}. Third, counterfactual comparison
makes the contribution of a single action learnable from a joint cost:
not only the immediate movement cost of one job but also the induced
waiting and equipment flow are evaluated in the same monetary unit.
Because the branch computation required for learning is separated from
operation, the decision path itself remains a small cost model and a
constraint check.

\subsection{Application and Future Directions}
Observations in the literature-calibrated continuous synthetic
environment indicate that the proposed structure carries spatio-temporal
reallocation through to execution, and that arrival-time adjustment and
learned selection can produce a direction of cost reduction, especially
under congested conditions. This supports the view that real-time
information at a smart port can lead to assignment actions rather than
monitoring alone~\cite{ref14,ref15,ref16}.

The same direction was observed when the action range was matched.
Adding rule-based policies that transfer the \emph{least-loaded}
criterion from the spatial to the temporal axis, arrival-time adjustment
lowered cost significantly even under a rule ($+3.41\%$), and the
proposed policy using the same actions was lower still (20/28,
$p=0.0357$). On the arrival-time axis alone, however, the rule-based
policy was lower on more days and the proposed policy led only in total.
The learned selection appears to have been optimised \emph{not towards
winning frequently but towards reducing cost sharply on the expensive
days}.

Subsequent development can refine one axis at a time while preserving
the core structure. Reflecting actual appointment quotas would make the
executable range of time adjustment concrete, and repeating a wider
variety of congested conditions would delineate the contribution
relative to the untrained model. Where long-horizon effects matter, a
method that redistributes reward over the whole decision sequence can
also be compared~\cite{ref23}.

\subsection{Concluding Remarks}
This paper organises observation, proposal, acceptance, commitment,
execution, and counterfactual learning into a single reproducible
structure. The experimental results indicate that this structure can
develop into a reinforcement learning policy that adjusts port space and
time together. As automated equipment and appointment information become
more widespread, the proposed architecture can serve as a basis for
connecting real-time congestion information to explainable resource
assignment decisions.
