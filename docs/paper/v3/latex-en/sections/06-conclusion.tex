\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:concl}

This paper proposed a reinforcement learning architecture that jointly
revisits the yard block and arrival time of an announced truck job during
operation. The architecture connects distributed appointment negotiation
~\cite{ref6} and yard-crane operations~\cite{ref7,ref8} in one
spatio-temporal assignment problem. Reallocation affects later truck,
crane, and vessel operations. Each policy therefore learns from a
counterfactual cost difference measured over a fixed horizon, rather
than from immediate movement cost. This is how multi-agent
counterfactual learning~\cite{ref10,ref11} enters the operating
decision. Expensive branch simulation is used only during offline
training. Online operation requires only small cost networks and
constraint checks.
In the synthetic environment of Sect.~\ref{sec:env}, the architecture carries
spatio-temporal reallocation from observation through proposal,
acceptance, commitment, and execution. Cost reductions arise mainly from
arrival-time adjustments on congested days. Real-time congestion information was therefore turned into assignment
actions and not only into monitoring. The learned policy also has lower total
cost than a rule with the same action range. For arrival-time adjustment
alone, however, the rule is lower on more days, while the learned policy
leads in total because of larger savings on congested days. Both daily
win counts and total savings are therefore needed for interpretation.

\subsection{Scope of the Study and Directions for Extension}
\label{sec:scope}
This study examined whether the proposed architecture operates as
intended, and what spatio-temporal reallocation is worth in cost terms,
in a synthetic environment built from terminal scales and operational
variation reported in the literature. The present results are therefore
better read as experimental evidence about the conditions under which
revisiting an existing assignment with congestion observed during
operation is effective, rather than as a direct performance estimate for
a particular terminal. Applying the arrival distribution, demand mix,
and cost data of an actual terminal would allow the range of
generalisation across different operating environments to be assessed
more concretely.
In this experiment the cost reduction concentrated in congested
conditions at high demand. This suggests extending the policy so that it
operates selectively according to the level of congestion rather than
continuously. Repeated evaluation across a range of demand patterns and
congestion frequencies would establish the conditions for such selective
intervention, and the effect of learning, more reliably.
On the operational side, the architecture should be extended to consider
the time-slot quotas of an actual appointment system, the procedures by
which carriers and the terminal revise an appointment, and a range of
crane dispatching rules. The open time-slot quota used here is a setting
for assessing the potential effect of arrival-time adjustment; applying
real quotas would define the executable action range of the policy more
realistically. The current separation of proposal and acceptance can
also be analysed in finer detail by comparing designs with and without
an acceptance veto and with different negotiation structures.
Methodologically, the influence of the three-hour counterfactual horizon
and of the cost coefficients on the relative value of policy actions can
be analysed further. In the present cost structure the share of truck
waiting cost is high, so arrival-time adjustment shows a large effect; a
sensitivity analysis over different cost structures and horizons would
therefore help identify the operating conditions under which spatial and
temporal reallocation are effective.
These extensions do not change the basic structure presented here
---observation, proposal, acceptance, commitment, execution, and
counterfactual learning---but carry it into real operating conditions
and a wider range of demand environments. This study therefore presents
one basic architecture for connecting real-time congestion information
to explainable resource-assignment decisions rather than to monitoring
alone, and it can develop into a validation that combines actual
terminal data with operational constraints.
