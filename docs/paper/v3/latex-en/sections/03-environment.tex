\section{Experimental Environment}\label{S:ExperimentalEn}

\subsection{A Literature-Calibrated Synthetic Environment}\label{S:ALiteratureCal}
The performance of the proposed reallocation architecture is verified
using numerical experiments. In order to conduct the experiments in a
more realistic setup, scales and operating ranges reported in existing
container terminal studies are employed to the extent possible. The
environment is therefore not a replica of the operating data of a
particular terminal but a \emph{literature-calibrated synthetic
environment}. Prior work supports the components as follows: 16 and 20
blocks~\cite{ref15,ref16}, several cranes per block~\cite{ref8,ref17},
multi-week continuous runs~\cite{ref9}, hourly arrival probability and its
effect on turn time~\cite{ref13,ref14}, three vessel classes with differing
call sizes~\cite{ref16,ref23}, and a triangular range for crane handling
time~\cite{ref9}. Within those ranges this study fixed 21 blocks, two
cranes per block, 30 days with state carried across the boundary, a base
plus three arrival peaks, small/medium/large vessels, and 180\,s of
crane service time.

The 21 blocks, the exact demand levels, and the arrival-curve
coefficients are therefore design values of this study. The citations
establish that the chosen scale and variation lie within the modelling
range of existing work; they do not indicate operating data from a
specific port.

\subsection{Terminal and Time Settings}\label{S:TerminalandTim}
The yard has 21 blocks with two cranes each (42 in total), a block
capacity of 1{,}440 slots at 65\% initial occupancy, and a
one-dimensional layout with the gate and the quay on opposite sides. The
decision interval is 60\,s, operation runs for 30 continuous days, and
the middle 28 days are compared. The crane work sequence gives priority
to service work and, within it, to the shortest expected processing
time. A day is a cut at which cost is separated, not a point at which
the terminal stops: inventory, queues, crane state, and unfinished jobs
carry over, with the first and last day connecting the boundaries.

\subsection{External Truck Demand and the Arrival Process}\label{S:ExternalTruckD}
The daily demand was sampled from a discrete distribution over
3{,}500, 5{,}000, 7{,}500, 12{,}500, and 15{,}000 trucks with
probabilities 0.30, 0.30, 0.25, 0.10, and 0.05. The first three levels
represent smooth, normal, and high demand; the last two represent
congested and heavily congested conditions. Within a day, the
announcement time of each job was drawn from the time-of-day arrival
density $f(t)$, a mixture of a uniform base and normal peaks:

\begin{equation}
f(t)=0.38\,U(0,24)+0.62\sum_{m=1}^{3}w_m
\mathcal N_{[0,24)}(t;\mu_m,\sigma_m^2),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
(\mu_m,\sigma_m,w_m)\in\{(10,1.5,.317),(15,2.5,.633),(21,1,.05)\}.
\end{equation}

Time is measured in hours and the truncated density is renormalised so
that it integrates to one over a day. The time-of-day arrival model and
its effect on turn time follow~\cite{ref13,ref14}; the mixture weights
and peak centres were selected as design values so that congestion
sensitivity can be compared across demand levels, and are not claimed to
be estimates fitted to a particular terminal. The uniform base keeps
arrivals non-zero at night, so the yard state carried
across the day boundary is not an artefact of an empty night.

\subsection{Vessels and Quay Operations}\label{S:VesselsandQuay}
Vessels were divided into three classes: small (50{,}000\,GT,
3{,}000\,TEU, two quay cranes, KRW 149{,}500 idle cost per hour), medium
(100{,}000\,GT, 7{,}500\,TEU, four, KRW 299{,}000), and large
(150{,}000\,GT, 14{,}000\,TEU, six, KRW 448{,}500). Here, the
parameters were generated as follows. The call volume of a vessel of
capacity $C$ TEU was sampled as $C\cdot u$ with $u\sim U[0.15,0.30]$. The daily composition was two small vessels and
one medium vessel, to which one large vessel was added with probability
0.30. The throughput of one quay crane was set to 27.5\,move/h, which
lies within the 24, 30, and 36\,move/h range used in the
literature~\cite{ref16}. Blocking across quay cranes was summed and
divided by the number assigned, giving the idle time per vessel.

\subsection{Units of Yard Crane Capacity}\label{S:UnitsofYardCra}
The reference service time of one crane is 180\,s per move, so the
nominal capacity is $\mu_{YC}=21\times2\times3600/180=840$
moves per hour. A move is a container movement, not a truck: one truck
job may require several moves and rehandles, and the realised service
time varies with position and job type. We therefore report move units
and truck units separately, following the guidance that capacity must be
stated with move type, automation delay, and handling-vehicle
conditions~\cite{ref18}.

\subsection{Cost Function}\label{S:CostFunction}
The total cost over the interval $[d,d+1)$ is the sum of four terms:

\begin{equation}
\Phi_d=C_{wait,d}+C_{move,d}+C_{rehandle,d}+C_{vessel,d}.
\end{equation}

Truck dwell costs KRW 40{,}000 per hour with the same rate applied again
beyond one hour, anchored on the safe trucking freight
rate~\cite{ref24}; vessel idling costs KRW 2.99 per GT-hour, converted
from the excess berthing rate of KRW 29.9 per 10\,GT-hour~\cite{ref25};
additional crane movement (KRW 60{,}000 per hour) and rehandling (KRW
2{,}000 each) are design values not taken from the literature. With
$h_i$ the dwell time of truck $i$, $m$ the additional crane working
time, $R$ the number of rehandles, and $T_v$ the policy-related idle
time of vessel $v$:

\begin{equation}
C_{wait}=\sum_i 40{,}000\{h_i+\max(0,h_i-1)\},\quad
C_{move}=60{,}000\,m,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
C_{rehandle}=2{,}000\,R,\quad C_{vessel}=\sum_v 2.99\,GT_v T_v.
\end{equation}

Structural vessel time that the reallocation policy cannot change, such
as berthing and quarantine, is separated as a diagnostic value. All four
terms are computed as differences of cumulative values at the same day
boundary.
